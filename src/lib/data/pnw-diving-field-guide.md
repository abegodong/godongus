# Diving the Pacific Northwest: Sharing My Experience with Newcomers

*A personal, eight-part account for divers making the leap into cold water — one diver's experience, offered to help rather than as the final word*

**By Abraham**

---

## Preface

Most people picture scuba diving as warm and blue: a coral reef somewhere tropical (Southeast Asia, the Caribbean, the South Pacific), a thin 3 mm wetsuit, or just shorts or a swimsuit, and sunlight streaming down to thirty feet. The Pacific Northwest is none of those things, and that is exactly why it is one of the most rewarding places on earth to dive. The Salish Sea is cold, green, and astonishingly alive.

<figure class="wrap-right">
<img src="img/community_dive.jpg" alt="A group of divers at the surface in Puget Sound">
<figcaption>A weekend shore dive in the Sound — part of the local community I had no idea existed until I went looking.</figcaption>
</figure>

I will admit that, like a lot of people, I had no idea any of this was here. Even after settling in Seattle, it took me a while to realize that Puget Sound has a genuine, vibrant diving community: shops, clubs, charters, and divers heading out to local shore sites every weekend of the year. When I mention that I dive here, the reaction is almost always the same: how can you possibly dive in water that cold, especially in winter? It is a fair question with a reassuring answer. The water temperature in the Sound is remarkably stable: it fluctuates far less than the air does, sitting in the upper 40s Fahrenheit essentially year-round.[^water-temp] The seasons that transform the weather barely move the water, so once you are equipped for it, a January dive is not meaningfully colder than a July one (and winter often brings the clearest water of all). That stability is a big part of what makes the leap from warm tropical diving to cold-water diving here far less daunting than it sounds.

Learning to dive the Sound well is less about depth and more about respect — for the temperature, for the tides, and above all for the limits of your own training.

That last point is the thread running through every part of this guide: **let your training lead the diving.** A certification card is not a trophy; it is permission for a specific environment and a specific equipment configuration that you have been trained to handle, and nothing beyond it. The divers who last in this region are the ones who treat that boundary as sacred and expand it deliberately, one course at a time.

This guide is written from exactly that path. My own diving started warm — open water certified in the tropics — and then moved north into the Sound one credential at a time, from drysuit and nitrox all the way to the divemaster rating. (The full route, and what each rung came to mean, is in Part 8.) Every one of those steps unlocked something new only *after* the training came first. That is the whole philosophy of cold-water diving in miniature.

**Who this is for.** I wrote this for five kinds of readers: warm-water divers who have moved to Seattle and are wondering whether Puget Sound is realistic; brand-new divers deciding whether to learn locally or somewhere tropical; certified divers coming back after years away; curious cold-water divers who want a practical map of the local community, gear, tides, sites, and next steps; and general readers who are not ready to dive the Salish Sea yet, but want to understand what it takes, what it feels like, and what this cold green world actually looks like. If you already dive the Sound, some of this will feel familiar. If you are new to it, my hope is that it gives you the map I wish I had.

The series is built as eight standalone parts that also read as a single guide:

1. **Why the Salish Sea** — the case for cold water.
2. **Getting Started** — three ways into local diving.
3. **Gear That Matters** — what to own, rent, and ignore at first.
4. **Find Your Community** — shops, clubs, and supported ways in.
5. **Reading the Water** — tides, slack, and conditions.
6. **Underwater Life** — the animals and encounters that make the cold worth it.
7. **Where to Dive** — local sites by difficulty.
8. **What Comes Next** — the training path beyond the basics.

**A quick note on three names.** I use three geographic terms in this guide, and they are not interchangeable. The **Pacific Northwest** is the broad land region (Washington, Oregon, and often British Columbia), and it is the familiar name on the cover. The **Salish Sea** is the water itself: the single inland marine ecosystem that runs from the south end of Puget Sound up through the San Juan Islands and the Strait of Juan de Fuca to the Strait of Georgia in British Columbia. The name was proposed by marine biologist Bert Webber and officially adopted by the United States in 2009 and Canada in 2010, as an umbrella that unites those waters and honors the Coast Salish peoples who have lived along them for millennia.[^salish] **Puget Sound** is the southern, Washington arm of the Salish Sea, running from Admiralty Inlet down to Olympia, and it is where I live, in Seattle, and where most of the diving in these pages takes place. So throughout, I use *Salish Sea* for these waters in general, and *Puget Sound* when I mean specifically my home stretch of them.

*One note before we begin: this guide is my own experience and opinion as a diver here, not professional instruction or a substitute for it. Diving carries real risk, and nothing in these pages replaces proper training, a certified instructor, current local conditions, and your own judgment. Wherever the two seem to conflict, defer to your training and the professionals at your shop.*

## Part 1 — Why the Salish Sea

### Summary

The Salish Sea offers world-class cold-water diving hidden in plain sight: 45–50°F water year-round, dramatic marine life found almost nowhere else, and a sense of genuine accomplishment that warm-water diving rarely demands. The cold is not an obstacle to tolerate but the price of admission to something extraordinary — and the first lesson it teaches is humility.

### In depth

The Salish Sea — the inland waters spanning Puget Sound, the San Juans, and the straits reaching toward the Pacific — is a temperate marine wilderness. The water sits between roughly 45 and 50 degrees Fahrenheit all year, which immediately reshapes what diving means here.[^water-temp] There is no casual splash in board shorts. Exposure protection is serious, planning matters, and conditions command attention. That single fact, the cold, is the root of nearly everything else in this guide.

<figure class="wrap-right">
<img src="img/wolf-eels_pair.jpg" alt="A pair of wolf eels in a rocky den">
<figcaption>A pair of wolf eels sharing a den — one of the Salish Sea's signature encounters.</figcaption>
</figure>

What you get in return is a cast of creatures that draws divers from around the world. The giant Pacific octopus, the largest octopus species on the planet, lives here in numbers. Wolf eels peer out of rocky dens with faces only a diver could love. Lingcod the length of your leg hold station over the reef, cabezon blend into the rocks, and plumose anemones bloom across pilings and wrecks like ghostly white gardens. Nudibranchs turn macro photography into an obsession, and on the right dive you may share the water with a sixgill shark or a curious harbor seal.

There is far more here than any one paragraph can hold, so I have given the local marine life its own part — see Part 6 for a proper introduction to who lives down there.

The counterintuitive reward, explored fully in Part 5, is that the cold season is often the *best* season. Winter brings reduced plankton blooms and some of the clearest visibility of the year, so the months most people assume are unbearable are frequently when the Sound shows off.

None of this is forgiving of ego. Cold water narrows your margins: thicker gear, heavier weighting, reduced dexterity, and conditions that change with the tide. The mindset that keeps divers safe here is the same one that makes them better — patience, preparation, and a clear-eyed sense of what you are and are not trained to do.

#### How I got hooked

The sea was in my life long before scuba diving was. I grew up in Jakarta, in a family that loved the water. My father and grandfather were fishermen at heart, and many of my childhood weekends were spent fishing the Java Sea with them. I took to skin diving early (I even acquired my own gear as a kid), and I spent a lot of that time wondering what the world looked like below the surface. What did the barracuda, octopus, stingrays, and the rest of the fish we hauled up actually *do* down there, in the part of the ocean I couldn't reach on a single breath? The question of what it would take to go deep, and stay, never quite left me.

It took decades to answer. The chance came on an impromptu trip to Bali, where a friend and I had gone to scout land for a yoga shala and wellness center we wanted to build. With some unexpected downtime, my brother-in-law, my sister's husband, suggested I finally get certified, and introduced me to a wonderful local PADI instructor. It was the right introduction. He was patient, deeply knowledgeable, attentive, an expert guide, and a gifted underwater photographer — exactly the kind of instructor worth seeking out if you are learning to dive in Bali.

Given my love of the water, I went straight into the Open Water course — skipping the Discover Scuba try-dive — and I was hooked from the first descent. I earned both my Open Water and Advanced Open Water certifications with him, and the childhood question was finally answered: I could go down, and stay, and watch the reef live its life around me.

What I didn't expect was the question that came next. Back home in Seattle, I started wondering what it would take to dive like this regularly — not on vacation, but in my own backyard. That question is what led me to the cold, green, world-class diving of the Salish Sea, and ultimately to this guide.

I am still learning, and still deeply fascinated by the beauty of what Puget Sound and the wider Salish Sea have to offer — not only the life underwater, but the diving community that has grown around it. I am not trying to rush through any of it. I am taking my time, letting each dive, each class, and each new connection pull me a little farther in, so I can be fully immersed in the place rather than simply passing through it.

Writing this stirs a warmth I didn't expect: it brings back happy memories from a childhood I hadn't revisited in a long time. People often ask me whether I'd rather be high up in the mountains or down low by the sea. I love the mountains, but my answer never changes: the sea. I have always been drawn to the water more than to anywhere else — and perhaps that is only fitting, since roughly 71 percent of the Earth's surface is covered by it.[^earth-water] The sea is most of the world, and to me it has always felt like home.

### Key takeaways

The Salish Sea is not warm-water diving with thicker neoprene. It is its own environment, with its own rhythm, rewards, and rules. The cold is manageable, the life is extraordinary, and the right mindset is humility: start supported, learn deliberately, and let the region reveal itself over time.

## Part 2 — Getting Started

### Summary

Almost everyone who takes up diving in the Salish Sea comes from one of three starting points: not yet certified, certified in warm water and now facing the cold, or certified long ago and returning after a hiatus. The right next step differs for each, but the underlying principle does not — match your training to the environment in front of you, and never get ahead of it. This part walks each path in turn, then closes with what the certification ladder itself has come to mean to me.

### In depth

**The first step I recommend, if you have not taken it, is to get properly trained.** People usually talk about "getting certified," but it helps to keep the order straight: the certification is just the result — the card that proves you have completed the training and can dive safely on your own. What actually matters is the training itself. Scuba diving is wonderful, but it is unforgiving: breathing compressed air underwater, and safely managing the pressure changes as you descend and come back up, takes real knowledge and practiced skill. You learn those fundamentals through a recognized training agency (the names you will hear most around here are PADI, SDI, and NAUI), and the card you earn at the end certifies that you can dive within set limits. Earn it once and it is yours for life.

It is worth being clear about *why* this matters, because it is easy to hear "you have to be certified" as a bureaucratic hurdle: a box to tick so a shop will fill your tank or rent you gear. It is not about that. The real reason is your own safety, and your own ego. The training gives you the judgment to manage yourself underwater and the humility to recognize your limits and stay inside them. The water does not care how confident you feel; trouble tends to find the diver who skips the fundamentals and trusts their nerve instead. Above all else, safety is the priority, and getting properly trained is simply the first and most important investment you make in it.

The entry-level certification is called Open Water Diver, and earning it means working through three things: the knowledge (a little theory on how diving affects your body and how to plan a safe dive), confined-water sessions in a pool to practice the core skills, and a handful of supervised dives in open water to put it all together. Once you hold that card, you can dive with a buddy, independent of an instructor, to recreational depths.[^open-water] Everything else in this guide builds from that first step, and the full progression beyond it is laid out in Part 8.

#### Choosing where and who to train with

Two things make choosing where to train far less stressful than it first looks.

The first is that the standards are essentially the same wherever you go. Recreational scuba training is governed by a global body — the World Recreational Scuba Training Council (WRSTC), founded in 1999 — that sets the minimum standards the major certifying agencies build their courses on, and many of those courses also meet international ISO standards.[^standards] In practice, a proper Open Water course teaches the same core skills and safety knowledge whether you take it in Bali or Bellingham, under one agency's flag or another's. The logo on your card matters far less than beginners fear.

It still helps to know the alphabet soup. PADI (Professional Association of Diving Instructors) is by far the largest agency in the world, so it is the name you will run into most. SDI (Scuba Diving International) is the recreational sister of TDI (Technical Diving International), an agency that grew out of technical diving and carries that rigor into its recreational courses. NAUI (National Association of Underwater Instructors) is one of the oldest, a nonprofit with a strong teaching reputation. There are others — SSI, RAID, GUE, and more — but those three are the ones you will meet most around Puget Sound. The differences between them are real but subtle, and for a new diver they matter far less than the shop and the instructor.

Which is the second reassuring thing: because the standards are shared, you do not need to agonize over the choice. Pick a reputable local shop that is convenient (you will go back often, so proximity counts) and just get started; any established Puget Sound shop will be set up for cold water, and you are not committing to it for life. Resist the urge to shop endlessly for the "perfect" instructor, especially for Open Water. What really determines whether you walk out genuinely competent is less the instructor and more how you engage with the course: read the materials closely instead of skimming, and ask questions about what you are learning. If something in the manual is unclear, raise it; if you want a skill demonstrated again, say so. You are never wrong to ask a question — a good course is a conversation, and that half of it is on you. So choose a shop quickly, then put your energy into the learning itself.

Read the material genuinely closely, because this is exactly where I went wrong myself. During my own Open Water course I skimmed the material, assuming the real learning would happen in the water — and I paid for it twice. Later on, the sheer range of gear and configurations out there confused me far more than it should have, because I had never built the foundation that makes sense of it all. And more immediately, I never properly learned to equalize my ears, one of the most basic and essential skills in diving, which left my early descents needlessly uncomfortable until I went back and actually understood the technique. The manual is where the fundamentals that keep you safe and comfortable live. Read it like it matters, because it does.

And you are never locked in. Your certifications are recognized across the whole industry, so you can do your Open Water at one shop and your next course at another (even under a different agency) and no one will blink. My own training is proof of it: my certifications span several agencies and at least five different shops. I earned my Open Water in Bali, and then, back home, spread my drysuit, nitrox, deep, solo, rebreather, scooter, and divemaster training across a handful of Seattle-area shops — each course building cleanly on the last, none of them concerned about which agency had issued the card before. Mix and match shops and agencies freely, and follow the instructors you trust.

With that established, here is the practical question. When people ask how to get started diving here, my first question back is always the same: where are you starting from? The answer sorts almost everyone into one of three paths.

1. **Not yet certified** — you want to learn to dive and are deciding where and how.
2. **Certified in warm water, moving to cold** — you hold a tropical card and want to dive the Salish Sea.
3. **Coming back after years away** — you were certified once, long ago, and life intervened.

The destination is the same for all three — safe, confident cold-water diving — but the road in is different. Here is how I would approach each.

#### Path 1 — Not yet certified

If you have never been certified, your first real decision is *where* to earn your Open Water certification: in warm water on a trip, or right here in the cold.

I will be honest about my own bias: I am glad I certified in the tropics first. Learning the fundamentals — clearing a mask, recovering a regulator, managing buoyancy, simply trusting that you can breathe underwater — is far easier in warm, clear, forgiving water than in 48-degree green water under a drysuit. Bali let me concentrate on the skills without also fighting the cold, and I think it made me a calmer, better student.

But credit where it is due: I have enormous respect for the divers who skip the gentle option and earn their certifications right here. Along the way I have met many newly minted local divers who completed their Open Water, Advanced Open Water, and Rescue courses entirely in Puget Sound — cold, drysuited, low-visibility, and slack-timed from the very beginning. That is the harder road, and the divers who take it come out genuinely prepared for home waters. Local shops make that path realistic: many build drysuit training right into their cold-water Open Water courses, so you learn in the suit you will actually dive.

So neither route is wrong. Certifying warm is the gentler way to learn the fundamentals; certifying here means you are cold-water ready from your very first ocean dive. What matters is that you train properly for wherever you actually intend to dive.

#### Path 2 — Certified in warm water, moving to cold

This is my path — and the one I got wrong at first.

Your Open Water card from a tropical destination is a recognized credential anywhere in the world; coming from Bali, you do not re-certify to dive the Salish Sea. But it is worth being precise about what that card represents: it certifies that you were trained to dive in the conditions and configuration you learned in — warm, calm, lightly weighted, in a thin wetsuit. It did not teach you cold-water exposure protection, drysuit handling, the heavier weighting cold diving requires, working with thick gloves, or planning a dive around the tide. **The card transfers; the competence for a new environment does not.** Recognizing that distinction is not a technicality — it is the single habit that keeps cold-water divers alive.

**My own false start.** When I came home from Bali, I was all gung-ho about diving the Salish Sea, so I did what felt obvious and went shopping. I bought a wetsuit — not my old 3 mm from the tropics, but a serious step up: a 7 mm farmer-john — and the full kit to go with it: a thick hood, heavy neoprene gloves, and boots. I was proud of the whole rig. To this day all of it is still hanging in my closet, having never touched the water.

The truth is that I was naive. I assumed I could suit up and wade in the way I had in the tropics, and that the only real difference was thickness. The Salish Sea does not work that way. Diving here calls for different tanks, different weighting, and an entirely different environment — one that rewards preparation and punishes the assumption that a warm-water habit will simply carry over. My expensive wetsuit became a monument to everything I didn't yet know.

That false start is a big reason this guide exists. I would rather you not fall through the same crack I did. If you are transitioning to the Salish Sea, my hope is that you ask the right questions, get the right introduction, and line up the right training and support *before* you stand at the water's edge — not after. It is also why I encourage newcomers to find a shop that offers a structured, drysuit-first start; the region has many excellent ones, covered in Part 4.

**The transition is an expansion, not a re-do.** You are deliberately widening your training to match a new environment. The biggest single piece of that is a gear decision: how you will stay warm, drysuit or wetsuit. Because it is tied so closely to the rest of your equipment, that decision has its own dedicated treatment in Part 3. The short version is that a wetsuit is a legitimate option, but I would point you toward a drysuit — it opens a whole different world and, importantly, requires its own dedicated training. Whichever you choose, the principle holds: get trained for it before you rely on it. (See *Exposure protection* in Part 3 for the full comparison and what each setup involves.)

**Re-entry, regardless of suit.** Coming off warm-water dives, and especially after any real layoff, the fastest way back is a couple of guided local dives or a shop orientation. Plenty of local shops run exactly this (guided dives, small-group outings, and charters), so you do not have to organize it yourself; Evergreen Dive Service, Seattle Dive Tours, and YSS Dive are a few that do, among others. You will recalibrate your weighting (a drysuit adds both an air space to manage and more lead to carry), get your buoyancy dialed in, and learn a specific site at slack before diving it on your own. If enough time has passed that you feel rusty, ask the shop specifically for its refresher course; just know that the exact name depends on the certifying agency. The three agencies you will encounter most among Puget Sound shops are PADI, SDI, and NAUI, and each has its own version: PADI's is **ReActivate**, SDI's is the **Inactive Diver / Refresher Program**, and NAUI's is **Refresher Scuba**.[^refresh] They cover the same ground, a knowledge review plus a supervised skills session in confined water, so the right one is simply whichever your shop teaches under. (More on returning after a long break in Path 3.)

#### Path 3 — Coming back after years away

A question I hear often is some version of this: "I got certified years ago — decades, in some cases — and I haven't dived since. What do I need to get back in the water? Is my certification even still valid?"

The reassuring part first: **your certification does not expire.** Open Water is a lifetime credential (PADI, SDI, NAUI, and the rest do not lapse), so the card you earned back in the 1990s is still good today. What lapses is not the certification but your *currency*: your skills, your comfort, and your muscle memory. That is why, after a long hiatus, the right next step is a refresher rather than starting over. Whatever agency you originally trained under, there is a refresher course built for exactly this. The main local ones (PADI ReActivate, SDI Inactive Diver/Refresher, NAUI Refresher Scuba) are described under Re-entry above; each walks you back through the knowledge and rehearses the core skills in a pool before you return to open water. Many dive operators will ask for a recent refresher, or proof of recent dives, before they rent you gear or take you on a guided dive. In the Salish Sea there is a second layer: if your original training was in warm water, coming back also means closing the cold-water and drysuit gap, so a returning diver here is often looking at a refresher *and* a drysuit course.

This is the part that has always worried me when I have been out of the water too long. It feels like skipping the gym for months: the muscle weakens, your readiness slips, and small mistakes start to creep in. They are rarely dramatic; more often they are the basic, easy-to-skip steps. Forgetting to connect the drysuit inflator hose. Forgetting the BCD inflator hose. Threading the regulator onto the tank before seating the BCD. None of these are exotic errors; they are exactly what goes wrong when a routine has gone rusty, and the longer I have been away, the more I worry about missing one of them. That worry is the whole case for a refresher. Whether you have been out for six months, a year, or far longer, a ReActivate class rehearses the routine until it is second nature again and gets you back in the water safely. It can also pull double duty as a jump-start into cold-water diving — a sensible on-ramp to getting comfortable in a wetsuit here before you go any further.

The other thing that has changed while you were away is the equipment, and it has changed a great deal. The fundamentals of diving are identical — the physics and physiology have not moved — but the gear has come a long way. Take the humble mask as an example. The one I owned in the 1990s worked perfectly well: it sealed, I could see, it did its job. A modern mask is simply better in every dimension. Skirts are now made of soft clear or black silicone that seals more reliably and lasts far longer than the rubber of decades past. Lenses are tempered, optically clearer, often anti-fog treated, and available in prescription. Frameless designs have made masks lighter and easier to pack.

The biggest leap for a returning diver to appreciate is the **low-volume mask.** A low-volume design holds less air inside, which means it sits closer to your face, gives a wider and more natural field of view, and, most usefully, is far easier to clear, because there is less air to displace when water gets in. Anyone who remembers wrestling with a big, bulky mask in their early training will find a modern low-volume mask a revelation. The same story repeats across the rest of the kit (dive computers, regulators, buoyancy systems, and exposure suits have all advanced enormously), but the mask is the easiest place to feel, immediately, how far things have come. (More on choosing gear in Part 3.)

### What I would do differently

If I were starting this path again, I would read the Open Water material more carefully, learn equalization properly from the beginning, and resist the urge to solve uncertainty by buying gear. I would take the cold-water transition as a real expansion of training, not just a change of wardrobe. And I would start with supported shop dives or a divemaster-led club before trying to join peer-organized dives, because the community is strongest when you enter it in the right order.

### Key takeaways

Start from where you actually are: new, warm-water certified, or returning after time away. If you are new, train well. If you are moving from the tropics, add cold-water competence rather than assuming the card covers everything. If you are rusty, take a refresher without ego. In every case, the goal is the same: match your training to the water in front of you.

## Part 3 — Gear That Matters

### Summary

Exposure protection, drysuit or wetsuit, is the gear decision that matters most for cold water, and it gets pride of place here. The rest of the menu — single tank, doubles, sidemount, rebreathers; standard or long-hose regulators; jacket BCDs versus backplate-and-wing; the endless mask, fin, and snorkel debates — looks like a set of equal choices but is actually a progression. Most of it solves problems beginners do not have yet. A configuration you are not trained for is not an upgrade; it is a hazard. The beginner's real decision set is small.

### In depth

I remember how genuinely confusing all of this was when I started. Walk into a dive shop and you are met with a wall of options: regulators lined up in a glass case, a rainbow of BCDs, tanks in different metals and sizes, and a vocabulary you do not yet speak. Head down to a popular shore site and it is somehow worse: divers are gearing up in wildly different rigs. One has a single aluminum tank and a familiar jacket BCD; the next is clipped into twin steel tanks on a metal backplate; another has cylinders mounted along the hips; and off to the side, someone descends in eerie silence, no bubbles at all. It is easy to look at that range and assume you are missing something, or that you must make a dozen expensive decisions before you can even get wet.

You do not. Here is the reassurance to hold onto: there is a standard recreational setup, and it is what the large majority of divers around you are actually using. A single tank, a BCD, a regulator with a primary second stage and an octopus backup, a mask, fins, exposure protection, weights, and a dive computer. That is a complete, capable kit: all you need to dive the Sound well. Everything else you see — the doubles, the sidemount, the rebreather, the long hose — is a later, optional branch off that same trunk, taken only when a particular kind of diving calls for it. Start from the standard setup, get comfortable, and let your diving tell you if and when you ever need more.

The cure for the overwhelm, in other words, is reframing: this is not a flat menu where every option is equally valid for you today. It is a ladder, and almost everything intimidating about it exists to solve problems that arrive much later — redundancy at depth, overhead environments, extended bottom times. Your job as a newcomer is to recognize where on the ladder you actually stand, and to climb it only as your training allows.

For readability, I think about the kit in five buckets:

1. **Warmth** — exposure suit, undergarments, hood, gloves, and boots.
2. **Buoyancy and trim** — BCD, drysuit gas, weighting, and tank choice.
3. **Gas delivery** — tank, regulator, hose routing, and eventually nitrox.
4. **Navigation and signaling** — computer, compass, SMB, spool, and light.
5. **Optional range and specialization** — DPVs, sidemount, doubles, cameras, and rebreathers.

The first two buckets matter immediately in the Sound. The last bucket can wait.

**Exposure protection: drysuit or wetsuit.** In 45-to-50-degree water this is the most important gear decision you will make (it determines whether a dive is a pleasure or an ordeal), so it comes first. The choice is between a drysuit and a wetsuit, and while a wetsuit is legitimate, I would point you toward a drysuit. It is also the one piece of exposure gear that comes with its own certification, because using it safely is a trained skill.

- *The drysuit route (what most locals do).* A drysuit seals you off from the water with watertight wrist and neck seals, keeping you dry and letting you tune your warmth through the undergarments worn beneath it. Using one safely is a trained skill, so it comes with its own certification: the PADI Dry Suit Diver specialty. The only prerequisite is an Open Water certification (it is open to divers as young as ten), it pairs a couple of hours of eLearning with in-water training, and the full course typically runs two to three days.[^drysuit] A regional nuance: in cold-water areas it is common to complete the Open Water course directly in a drysuit rather than adding the skill later. Drysuit certification is offered at essentially every serious shop in the area (Evergreen Dive Service, Lighthouse Diving Center, and Underwater Sports among them), and some build it straight into their Open Water courses. It is not legally required, but nearly every local shop expects it before renting you a suit, and it is the difference between enjoying a 48-degree dive and merely surviving it.
- *The wetsuit route (the honest alternative).* Diving the Sound wet is possible, but it is a real commitment to being cold. The setup is a 7 mm semi-dry suit as a base, layered with a hooded vest so your core effectively carries double thickness. Expect shorter, colder dives and reduced dexterity. Most divers who intend to keep diving here migrate to a drysuit within a season, which is why the gear math usually favors going dry sooner rather than later — and if you do buy a wetsuit, actually dive it, or it ends up like mine, a 7 mm ornament in the closet.
- *The extremities, either way.* Whichever suit you choose, your head and hands lose heat and dexterity fastest, so a proper hood, gloves (thick 5–7 mm wet gloves, or dry gloves integrated into a drysuit), and boots are not afterthoughts — they make or break a cold dive.

**I am in a drysuit and I am still too cold — now what?** First, make sure the basics are right: no leaks, seals that fit, enough gas in the suit to remove squeeze, dry gloves or warm wet gloves, a proper hood, and a dive plan that matches your actual cold tolerance. But even with all of that sorted, some divers still run cold. I have tried every major route to getting warmer in a drysuit — thicker undergarments, heated gear, and argon inflation — and for me, all of them helped. A drysuit keeps you dry; the warmth mostly comes from what you put under it, how well the whole system traps heat, and how carefully you tune it to the dive you are actually doing.

The first adjustment is usually **thicker undergarments**. Drysuit insulation comes in many weights, from light base layers for mild days to thick 300-gram or 400-gram lofted undergarments for colder water and longer dives. Thicker is warmer because it traps more air around your body, but it also adds bulk and buoyancy, which means more lead, more drag, and a weighting check before you trust it. This is why many local divers eventually own more than one undergarment setup: lighter layers for easier dives, heavier layers for winter, deeper profiles, or longer bottom times.

The next step is **active heat**: heated vests, heated undergarments, heated gloves, or full heated systems powered by a battery canister. I have used the Venture Heat Dry V3 heated vest, and even before turning it on, the material itself helped keep me warm. Once powered, it made a real difference — but it is still a vest, so it mainly warms the core. That matters enormously, but it does not directly warm your arms or legs. There are also heated diving undersuits, such as the Santi BZ400 heated undersuit, that I have not tried yet; those are built to warm more of the body, from the shoulders down through the arms and legs.[^thermal-protection] Active heat can make a huge difference, especially on long dives or repetitive cold-water days, but it is not magic and it is not a substitute for passive insulation. Batteries fail, connectors flood, and heat can stop mid-dive, so you still need enough non-heated insulation to finish the dive safely if the system quits. Treat heat as a comfort and margin booster, not as the only thing keeping you warm.

Some divers also use **argon gas for drysuit inflation**. Argon conducts heat less readily than air, so in theory it can reduce heat loss through the gas space inside the suit. You do not need an extra certification just to use argon as suit inflation gas, but you do need to treat the bottle correctly: it is not breathing gas. Do not put a breathing regulator on an argon bottle, do not breathe from it, and make sure it is clearly set up and labeled as suit gas only. Argon is inert and non-toxic, but it does not contain the oxygen your body needs; breathing it can cause hypoxia, unconsciousness, and death.[^argon-safety] Because it is denser than air, divers also worry about it being harder to clear if it is accidentally breathed, which is another reason the rule should be simple and absolute: never breathe argon.

Argon for warmth is also controversial. Some divers vouch for it; others think the benefit is too small to justify the extra cylinder, regulator, fills, and cost. Part of the trick, if you use it, is to get as much regular air out of the suit as practical before adding argon, because the benefit depends on the gas in the suit actually being argon rather than a diluted mix of argon and air.[^argon-suit] I will not pretend the debate is settled, but I can say this: I have used it, and I personally found that it helped. Locally, shops that can fill argon include Silent World, Eight Diving, Anacortes Diving Supply, and YSS Dive; pricing varies by shop and fill. A small 6-cubic-foot argon bottle will usually last about two to four dives, depending on suit volume, depth changes, how much gas you add, and how carefully you purge and manage it. For most new cold-water divers, though, I would still solve the obvious problems first — better undergarments, better gloves, and possibly active heat — before adding another gas bottle to manage.

The simple order I would try is this: fix leaks and extremities first, upgrade undergarments second, consider heat if you are doing longer or repetitive dives, and leave argon for the point where the added complexity actually makes sense. And whenever you change warmth systems, redo your weighting and buoyancy practice. Warmer gear almost always changes how you float.

**Why that training is not optional.** A drysuit is, in effect, a sealed pocket of air wrapped around your whole body, and that air moves with you. Tip even slightly feet-up and it slides down into your legs and boots, where there is no valve to let it out. Your feet start to rise, you can flip inverted, and an untrained diver can be pulled into a fast, feet-first, runaway ascent. That is genuinely dangerous: an uncontrolled ascent risks lung-overexpansion injuries and decompression sickness. Learning to manage that air — adding just enough to take the squeeze off, venting it steadily as you rise, and holding your trim — is the entire reason the course exists.

I felt every bit of this on my first few dives after getting certified. I would get "floaty feet" — air sliding down into my boots and lifting them toward the surface — and find myself riding up and down the water column while I learned to control the air in the suit. What kept me in command of it was the drysuit training, not nerve and not luck: knowing how much air to add, how to dump it, and how to recover the instant my feet went light. Untrained, I would have been a hazard to myself.

The suit is gear, but diving it safely is training; Part 2 covers *when* in your journey to make this choice.

**Cylinder configurations — a ladder of redundancy and complexity.**

- **Single tank.** Where everyone starts and where most recreational divers happily stay. The only real beginner sub-choice is aluminum versus steel, which matters enough in the Salish Sea to be worth covering on its own, just below.
- **Back-mounted doubles (twinset).** Two cylinders manifolded together for redundancy and gas volume. This is technical territory, where an introductory course like Tec 40 begins to formalize gas planning and decompression thinking. Not a recreational starting point.
- **Sidemount.** Cylinders clipped along your sides instead of your back. Born in cave diving, it is prized for flexibility, easier transport, and disciplined gas management. A deliberate later choice, not a default.
- **Closed-circuit rebreather (CCR).** A rebreather recycles your exhaled gas, scrubs out the carbon dioxide, and adds oxygen as needed. The payoff is near silence, no bubbles, and dramatically extended dive times. The cost is real money, intensive training, heavy task-loading, and far less tolerance for error. This sits near the very top of the ladder for good reason; it is a multi-year aspiration for most divers, approached only through dedicated training such as the CCR courses offered locally at Silent World.

**A personal note on all this.** I have trained on much of that ladder, sidemount and closed-circuit rebreather included, and I am glad I did. But for everyday recreational diving, I keep coming back to the simplicity of a single tank. It is the most practical setup there is for the kind of diving most of us do most of the time: less to rig, less to fail, less to manage, and more attention left over for the dive itself. The catch is that the other configurations demand upkeep: skills and muscle memory fade if you do not use them. So I make a point of getting back on sidemount and the rebreather regularly just to keep them sharp. The lesson I would pass on is simply this: do not rush up the ladder. The single tank is not a beginner's consolation prize; it is exactly what buddy diving was designed around: two divers in simple, matched gear, each able to help the other. It will serve you beautifully for as long as you dive.

**Steel or aluminum — and how big?** The quick answer I learned for Salish Sea diving: go steel. In practice, a steel 100 is the tank people here dive again and again.

Here is the reasoning. For a single-tank recreational diver, the one tank choice that genuinely matters is the metal, and it comes down to buoyancy. The aluminum 80, or "AL80" (the classic rental tank you most likely learned on, which despite its name actually holds about 77 cubic feet of air), begins the dive slightly negative but swings several pounds *positive* as you breathe it down, so near the end of a dive, exactly when you are trying to hold a calm safety stop near the surface, the empty tank is working to float you up. A steel tank stays negative, or close to it, from full to empty, never developing the strong positive pull of an empty aluminum tank. That stability is worth a great deal, and there is a bonus: because the steel tank carries some of your ballast itself, you can take an equivalent amount of lead off your weight belt. In a drysuit, which already demands a lot of lead, that means a lighter belt, better balance, and easier trim. It is why most committed local divers dive steel.

Here is how a few common tanks compare — capacity alongside how buoyancy shifts from a full tank to an empty one:

| Tank | Capacity (cu ft) | Buoyancy, full (lb) | Buoyancy, empty (lb) | Full→empty swing (lb) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Aluminum 80 (AL80) | ~77 | −1.5 | +3.5 | ~5.0 |
| Steel HP80 | 82 | −8.1 | −1.7 | 6.3 |
| Steel HP100 | 101 | −8.4 | −0.6 | 7.8 |
| Steel HP117 | 118 | −9.1 | +0.4 | 9.5 |
| Steel HP133 | 136 | −9.1 | +1.5 | 10.5 |

*Representative figures (steel from Faber's high-pressure line, aluminum from a standard AL80); exact numbers vary by manufacturer and fill, measured in salt water with a valve. Two things to read from it: bigger tanks hold more gas but have a larger buoyancy swing, because there is more gas weight to burn off over the dive; and the real virtue of steel is not a smaller swing but where it ends up — at or near negative when empty, instead of the firmly positive aluminum tank that tries to float you off your safety stop. The advantage is cleanest in the HP80–HP100 range; the big 117s and 133s do drift slightly positive when empty, though nowhere near as much as aluminum.*[^tanks]

**Weighting for a drysuit.** Weighting is the other half of that equation, and it is worth getting right rather than guessing. You are properly weighted when you can hold a stop at around 15 feet, neutrally buoyant, with a nearly empty tank and almost no air in your BCD or drysuit. A drysuit adds buoyancy (from the air inside it and from the undergarments), so cold-water divers carry noticeably more lead than they would in the tropics. A good starting point is roughly 10 percent of your body weight in lead, and then you adjust from there. Treat that only as a first estimate to get you in the ballpark, not a final number, because the real figure depends on your body, your suit, your undergarments, and your tank — and with thick undergarments you may well end up above it. Confirm it with a proper weight check, done with an instructor or experienced buddy and ideally during your drysuit course or a shop orientation, and write down what works. Your tank feeds straight into it: switch from aluminum to steel and you will shed a few pounds of lead.

As for size, tanks run from the AL80 up through steel tanks of roughly 80, 100, 117, and 133 cubic feet. How much gas you actually need depends on how much you breathe, how deep you go, and how hard you work, and cold-water diving pushes all of that up, so you will generally go through more air here than you did on a warm reef.

It is worth understanding why, because the difference is real and it directly drives tank choice. The biggest factor is simply staying warm: water pulls heat from your body far faster than air (on the order of twenty times faster), so even in a good drysuit your body is constantly burning energy to hold its core temperature. That raised metabolism demands more oxygen, which shows up as faster, deeper breathing throughout the dive, even when you are barely moving.

On top of that, you are working harder to get around: a drysuit, thick undergarments, hood, gloves, and the extra lead they require all add weight and drag, so every fin kick costs more than it did in a thin tropical wetsuit. You also feed gas from your tank into the drysuit (and your BCD) to offset squeeze and manage buoyancy, so a share of your supply goes into your suit rather than your lungs. The conditions themselves add to it: cold, dark, lower visibility, current, and unfamiliar heavy gear quietly raise your stress and your breathing rate, especially on your first cold-water dives, before growing comfort settles it back down. Layer on the fact that many local dives are deeper, where each breath draws proportionally more gas, and it is easy to see why a tank that lasted an hour on a warm reef empties noticeably faster here.

For most Puget Sound shore diving, a single steel 100 (an "HP100") is the comfortable sweet spot and a great place to start: meaningfully more gas than an AL80, with the steady negative buoyancy you want. It is normally good for a 40-minute to one-hour dive, about the length of an average recreational dive here in the Sound. The HP100 is also one of the most common tanks in the region, which is a real practical advantage: if your needs change, it is easy to sell and swap: up to a 117 or 133 if you find you want more gas, or down to an 80 if you turn out to sip air. The choice is low-stakes either way. The larger 117s and 133s suit divers who breathe more or want longer dives, at the cost of more weight and money.

Rent or own? Rent while you are new. Rental tanks are cheap, need no upkeep, and let you try different sizes before committing — and a regional quirk works in your favor here: most local shops rent steel, a steel 80 or 100, so the tank you learn on is already the right kind for cold water. (The aluminum 80 mostly turns up in the pool for confined-water sessions.) Owning starts to make sense once you dive often enough that the convenience pays for itself — you control your fills, always have a tank ready, and can settle on the size and type that fits you. Owned tanks do need care: an annual visual inspection and a hydrostatic test every five years.

**Where to start.** If that is a lot to take in, here is the short version: while you are learning, just dive the steel tanks your shop rents you (locally that usually means a steel 80 or 100) and put your attention on your skills. When you are ready to own your first tank, get a single steel 100. It is the tank most Puget Sound divers would steer a newcomer toward, and it will serve you well for years.

**Regulators and hose routing.** Beginners learn on a standard recreational setup with a primary second stage and an octopus backup. As divers advance, many adopt the long-hose ("Hogarthian," or DIR) configuration: the primary regulator on a long hose that you donate to a buddy in an emergency, with your own backup on a bungee necklace. It originated in technical and overhead diving, but its logic — clean gas-sharing in low-visibility, single-file situations — translates well to the murky Sound. A genuinely local concern at every level is the regulator's cold-water rating: environmentally sealed regulators resist the free-flows and freezing that cold water can trigger.

**Buoyancy: jacket versus back-inflate versus backplate-and-wing.** A jacket BCD wraps around you and is the typical rental — intuitive and forgiving for new divers. A back-inflate BCD moves the bladder behind you for better trim and is a common middle step. The backplate-and-wing (BPW) is a modular system: a metal or aluminum backplate, a harness, and a separate wing bladder.

There is a good reason most shops put Open Water students in jackets rather than a backplate-and-wing. A jacket's wraparound bladder floats a new diver upright and face-up at the surface, which feels secure and stable (exactly what a nervous beginner, or a choppy entry, calls for), and the familiar vest shape, built-in weight pockets, and easy strap adjustment make it simple to don and to fit across a whole range of students from a single rental fleet. It is also cheaper and the near-universal rental worldwide, so learning on one means you can step onto almost any dive boat and use what is on the rack. For a student whose entire job is to get comfortable breathing underwater, that forgiving simplicity matters far more than fine trim.

Once the basics are second nature, the backplate-and-wing pulls ahead. Because the bladder sits behind you and the metal plate adds ballast on your back, a BPW encourages a flat, horizontal trim rather than the slightly head-up posture a jacket tends to produce, and its streamlined profile creates less drag, which means easier finning and lower gas consumption. It is modular and rebuildable part by part, and it carries heavy steel tanks, doubles, and drysuit weighting far better than a jacket, which is why so many local and technical divers migrate to it. The trade-off is exactly what makes it less ideal on day one: a BPW gives less surface flotation and tends to float you face-down, so it rewards the body awareness you build over your first dozens of dives rather than handing it to you for free.[^bcd] None of this is a beginner emergency — a rental jacket is a perfectly good place to learn, and the move to a backplate-and-wing is one to make when you are ready, not before.

**The "fit beats features" trio.** For masks, fins, and snorkels, fit and suitability matter far more than spec sheets. A **mask** that leaks ruins every dive, so fit comes before everything; low volume and single- versus twin-lens are secondary. **Fins** have a real cold-water fork: open-heel fins worn over drysuit or wetsuit boots rather than full-foot fins, and stiffer blades (the classic heavy rubber "jet" style) that suit drysuit diving and a frog kick over a silty bottom, as opposed to split fins. Spring straps are the quality-of-life upgrade everyone eventually buys. The **snorkel** is genuinely optional: it is required during some training, but many local and technical divers leave it behind to reduce drag and entanglement risk, with a folding pocket snorkel as the compromise.

**Propulsion — the DPV.** A diver propulsion vehicle, or underwater scooter, extends your range and turns a long surface-current swim into a non-event. It is a wonderful tool and, like everything else here, a *trained* skill: a course such as the DPV training offered at YSS Dive exists precisely because a scooter in untrained hands is a fast way to get into trouble.

**So what does a beginner actually buy?** Own the personal-fit items first — a mask, then fins and boots, then exposure protection — and rent the rest until your diving tells you what you need. Buy as your diving leads you, not the other way around. The honest truth is that most newcomers never need anything past a single tank, a back-inflate or backplate-and-wing, and a sealed cold-water regulator. Everything else on that intimidating wall is a destination, not a starting line.

**And when you buy, shop locally.** It is tempting to take a shop's fitting advice and then order the same gear online for a few dollars less, but I would urge you not to. The price difference buys you far more than it seems: a local shop fits the gear to *you*, services what it sells, fills your tanks, answers the question you did not know to ask, and stands behind the purchase when something needs adjusting before a weekend dive. More than that, these shops are the infrastructure of the whole community you will read about in the next part — the training, the rentals, the air fills, the shop dives, the clubs. Every purchase keeps that ecosystem alive, and the relationship you build with your shop will pay you back many times over in advice, priority, and trust. Online retail cannot fill your tank on a Friday afternoon.

To pull it together, here is the kit I would point a newcomer toward for the Sound, based on my own experience — what is essential, what is worth having, and what to rent or skip at first:

| Gear | Recommended for the Sound | Buy or rent first | Necessity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mask | Low-volume, well-fitting, anti-fog | Buy (personal fit) | Essential |
| Fins | Open-heel over boots, stiff blade, spring straps | Buy | Essential |

**My own current primary single-tank kit, for the curious.** Since people ask, here is what I actually dive. None of this is sponsored — every piece was bought at full price or on the discounts local shops regularly run (one more reason to shop locally, stay on their mailing lists, and regularly check their social media for the specials they post):

- **Mask and fins:** Hollis M1 mask; Hollis F1 fins — the classic heavy rubber blade mentioned above.
- **Exposure protection:** Bare X-Mission Evolution drysuit with built-in boots; DUI DuoTherm II 300 undergarment; Fourth Element Arctic socks; Bare 7 mm Ultrawarmth dry hood; Waterproof Ultima dry gloves with Fourth Element glove liners.
- **Buoyancy:** Halcyon Eclipse single-tank backplate-and-wing with a stainless steel plate, weighted single-tank adapter, and integrated ACB weight pockets — the steel plate and adapter carry ballast on my back, exactly the lead-off-the-belt logic from the tank section.
- **Regulators:** Apeks DST first stage with an XTX50 second, in the long-hose configuration — the DST is environmentally sealed, the cold-water requirement covered above.
- **Computer:** Suunto Nautic with Tank POD air integration.

One more thing worth noticing about that list: it is a thorough mix-and-match — Hollis, Bare, DUI, Fourth Element, Waterproof, Halcyon, Apeks, and Suunto all in one rig — and that is perfectly OK. Dive gear is not a matched set, and there is no prize for brand loyalty. What matters is that each piece is the right tool for its job, that it fits *you*, and that everything works together as a system you have tested and trust. I share it not as a shopping list but as one worked example of the principles in this part: sealed cold-water regulators, steel-backed buoyancy, layered dry exposure protection, and a configuration adopted one trained step at a time. Your kit should be fitted to you, by your shop.

### Key takeaways

Buy personal-fit gear first, rent the expensive life-support pieces until your diving pattern is clear, and do not climb into configurations you have not trained for. For most new Salish Sea divers, the practical starting point is simple: drysuit training, a properly weighted setup, rented steel tanks, a sealed cold-water regulator, and enough patience to let your kit evolve with your experience.

## Part 4 — Find Your Community

### Summary

Before you ever plan a dive of your own, this is how you actually get in the water — through the community, in a sensible order. There is a natural progression to it: from divemaster-supported shop dives and shop clubs, where someone experienced is looking after you, to the peer-run dive clubs and Facebook groups, where you become the buddy others rely on. Climbing it in order is how you build real local competence — and how you avoid getting in over your head, as I once did.

### In depth

The hardest practical problem in diving the Salish Sea is not gear or cold; it is finding experienced people who will dive a current-sensitive site at the right slack window and help you grow safely. The community here is built for exactly that, and there is a sensible order in which to plug into it, each step a little less supervised, and a little more dependent on you, than the one before. You climb it the same way you climb everything else in this guide: in order, never skipping ahead of what you are ready for.

**Dive shops — your home base.**[^local-community]

The region has a deep bench of excellent shops, and my own training touched many of them. I earned my drysuit and nitrox certifications at **Underwater Sports**, my Deep specialty at **Argonaut Diving**, solo diving and closed-circuit rebreather training at **Silent World Diving Systems** (strongly cold-water focused), and a diver propulsion vehicle course at **YSS Dive** on Hood Canal. I have been working toward my divemaster at **Evergreen Dive Service** in Everett, a PADI 5 Star facility that, like a number of local shops, builds drysuit training into its Open Water courses so new divers learn in the suit they will actually use. Other long-standing options include **Lighthouse Diving Center** in Lynnwood, a regional fixture since 1972, and **Eight Diving** in Des Moines, known for GUE courses and a path into technical diving. Drysuit certification in particular is something you can get at essentially any serious shop in the area.

Choosing among them is the same exercise described in Part 2: because the standards are shared, pick the shop that fits your life rather than agonizing over the brand: proximity matters most, since you will visit often, and an attached club is a bonus. The best shop is simply the one that becomes your community.

**Step 1 — Shop dives, open to anyone.** After your Open Water course or a ReActivate refresher (or even a Discover Local Diving outing with a shop like Evergreen Dive Service, Seattle Dive Tours, or YSS Dive), the gentlest way to start diving locally is a shop dive. Many shops run regular outings that are open to the public, so you can simply sign up and join. Evergreen Dive Service, for instance, runs monthly "donut dives" and a more advanced "TGIF dive," both open to anyone who wants in; Underwater Sports runs similar free shop dives. These make ideal first community dives: a divemaster on staff runs the dive, with seasoned divers in the water alongside you, so even as a drop-in you are looked after the whole time.

**Step 2 — Shop clubs, with a divemaster on every dive.** A step up in structure are the membership-based clubs that some shops run. Silent World (in Bellevue) and Anacortes Diving (in Anacortes) each have their own shop club. Like the public shop dives, these keep a divemaster on every dive; what membership adds is a regular rhythm of outings, a community invested in helping newly certified local divers feel safe and dial in their skills for Puget Sound while they build experience, and a set of genuinely useful perks — discounted local boat dives, discounted dive travel, pool time to practice skills, and club events that keep the community connected between dives.

**Step 3 — Dive clubs and Facebook groups, where you become the buddy.** Once you genuinely feel comfortable in the water, the wider community opens up. **Marker Buoy Dive Club** is the big tent: it reports more than 270 members and runs roughly 150 local dives a year, mostly shore dives around the Sound, plus charters and some international travel; membership spans the newly certified to divers with thousands of dives, and it will deliberately pair a new member with a strong buddy.[^local-community] The **Emerald Sea Dive Club** is a more specialized, enthusiast-oriented community. Facebook-based groups lower the barrier further still: **Bubbles & Suds** and **Kelp Krawlers** for finding people and ad-hoc dives, **Thursday Night Diving at Redondo** as a recurring, just-show-up meetup at a beginner-friendly site, and the **GUE Community Dive**, which, true to its name, is open to anyone, whatever equipment you dive. It is a relaxed way to see up close how GUE divers configure and wear their kit (the long-hose, backplate-and-wing approach from Part 3), and if you like what you see, a natural first step toward getting trained with GUE yourself. The crucial difference from the steps before: these often do not put a divemaster on every dive. They run on the buddy system — which means you are no longer only being looked after; someone is now counting on you.

**A week's worth of diving.** I learned just how alive this community is during a stretch when I set out to log forty dives in a single month. I joined essentially everything — every club and community group I could — not to grind numbers for their own sake, but to immerse myself in what the Salish Sea has to offer, and I came away convinced there is a genuine place for every one of these on-ramps.

Between them, you can dive nearly every day of the week. Weekends almost always have something going, tides permitting. Midweek fills in fast: YSS Dive runs a Tuesday-morning community dive that regulars still fondly call "Taco Tuesday"; the GUE Community Dive runs every Wednesday; also on Wednesday, Underwater Sports Tacoma hosts its "Mudshark" two dives; the Thursday Night Diving at Redondo group on Facebook puts a dive in the water most Thursday evenings; and most months Evergreen Dive Service runs its TGIF dive to close out the week on Friday. Once you are plugged in, the hardest part is no longer finding a dive; it is choosing which one.

And it compounds: the more you show up, the more divers you get to know, and over time you build a real circle of buddies who share your availability — the people you come to depend on to get in the water with you. I had set out to do forty dives that month; with this much on offer, along with a buddy who shared my availability, I cleared fifty without much trouble.

**Step 4 — Eventually, you host.** Here is the part newcomers rarely see coming: in the dive clubs, the dives are *member-hosted*. There is no paid guide quietly running things: any member can host a dive, and as you settle in, the community will count on you to take your turn. It is how the whole system keeps working, and it is genuinely rewarding; it also raises the stakes, because now other divers are showing up on a plan that you made. That is why knowing how to plan a dive well — the site, the slack window, the conditions, the contingencies — matters so much, and it is exactly what the next part takes up.

**Why the order matters.** This progression is not bureaucracy; it is the buddy system taken seriously. On a community dive your buddy depends on you exactly as much as you depend on them, and you owe it to them to be genuinely ready before you take that on. I learned this the hard way: only a few dives after earning my drysuit certification, I jumped into a dive that was well beyond me at the time, and it took a more senior diver pulling me aside afterward to make me see what I had been asking of the people next to me.

The honest truth is that I did not do it out of bravado — I simply did not know any better. Nobody had laid out the on-ramps for me; I had no idea the supported shop dives and divemaster-led club dives even existed, or that there was a sensible order to climb. So when I wanted to get in the water, the dive I happened to find was one I had no business being on yet. That gap — the missing map — is a big part of why I wanted to write this in the first place: so the progression is obvious to you in a way it was not to me, and you can climb it deliberately instead of stumbling past the safe steps the way I did.

Build up through the supported steps first. The self-reliant ones will still be there when you are ready — and you will be a far better buddy for having waited.

**Your safety is always yours to protect.** On a community dive, safety is paramount, and that is the other half of being a good buddy: looking after yourself honestly. Before you sign on, assess your own skills against what the dive actually demands, and join only the dives that are genuinely within your capability — not the ones you wish you were ready for. Once you are there, remember that any diver can call any dive, at any time, for any reason. If you do not feel comfortable, or you see something off in how the dive is being run, in the timing of the slack window, or in the sea conditions, you abort — full stop. There is no embarrassment in thumbing a dive. That is what competent, trusted divers do, and the people worth diving with will respect you for it.

### Your first 90 days

A concrete on-ramp that pulls the whole series together:

1. **Train for the environment.** Book Open Water if you are starting fresh, or the drysuit specialty if you are already certified from warmer water.
2. **Sort exposure protection and the basics (Part 3).** Decide drysuit versus wetsuit, and budget for what to own versus rent.
3. **Plug into the community, in order.** Start with shop dives and a divemaster-led shop club; move to the peer clubs and Facebook groups once your skills are genuinely solid.
4. **Learn to read the water (Part 5).** Practice slack-tide planning until it is second nature.
5. **Progress deliberately (Part 7).** Dive the easiest Tier 1 sites first, then move up a tier only as your experience and training genuinely support it.

### Your diving progression

If I had to turn all of this into a simple progression, I would make the early phase boring on purpose. This is not a five-dive checklist, and it is not a race. Some divers may move through these steps quickly; others may spend a full season on the first few. The pace matters far less than the outcome: calm, repeatable comfort in local water.

1. **Start with supported orientation dives.** Go with a local shop, guide, or divemaster-led club at Cove 2 or another easy site. Let the goal be weighting, comfort, and learning the cold-water routine.
2. **Repeat easy sites until the basics settle.** Do not rush to novelty. Repeating a site lets you notice what changed: buoyancy, trim, gas use, cold, visibility, and how your body feels after the dive.
3. **Add simple navigation only when comfort is steady.** Swim modest out-and-back routes, keep the plan small, and make clean returns to the exit before you add current, depth, or complexity.
4. **Join supported community dives.** Meet more local divers while there is still structure around the day, and learn how experienced people brief, enter, turn, exit, and call dives.
5. **Begin planning easy dives with your buddy.** Pick forgiving sites, check the current and conditions, talk the plan through with someone who knows the place, and only then dive it.
6. **Move up only when the previous step feels uneventful.** That is the real signal. If gearing up, descending, staying warm, holding trim, navigating, and exiting still consume all your attention, stay where you are and keep practicing.

After enough dives like that, you will know far more than any gear purchase or internet thread can teach you. You will know how the cold feels on *you*, how much lead you actually need, how quickly you breathe, and what kind of support helps you relax enough to learn.

### Key takeaways

The community is part of the safety system. Start where someone experienced is watching the edges of the day, then move toward peer dives as your own competence catches up. The goal is not to be dependent; it is to become the kind of buddy other divers can depend on.

## Part 5 — Reading the Water

### Summary

In the Salish Sea, difficulty is defined by current, not depth. The core local skill is reading tides and currents and timing dives around slack water — the brief windows when current drops near zero. Visibility is often best in fall and winter. Honest go/no-go decisions and turning a dive around are not failures; they are what diving within your limits looks like in practice.

### In depth

Warm-water diving teaches you to think about depth and bottom time. The Sound demands a different instinct: watch the water move. Many of the best sites here sit in or near channels where tidal currents can run faster than any diver can swim. Get the timing right and the dive is serene; get it wrong and it ranges from exhausting to genuinely dangerous. This is the competency that separates a cold-water diver from a warm-water diver holding a cold-water card.

**Slack tide diving.** The key concept is slack water: the short period when the current pauses between flooding and ebbing. Crucially, tide tables and current predictions are two different things; the height of the water and the speed of the current are independent, and you plan dives around the *current* prediction at the relevant station, not simply high or low tide. Slack is not a single fixed clock time, either: it is corrected against reference current stations, and the size of the tidal exchange determines how forgiving (or unforgiving) the window will be. A small exchange gives a long, gentle slack; a large exchange gives a short, sharp one bracketed by fast water.[^tides]

**Visibility and seasonality.** The Salish Sea's worst-kept secret is that winter often delivers the best diving. Plankton blooms that cloud the water in spring and summer fade in the colder months, and visibility opens up accordingly. Rain runoff, recent storms, and a site's exposure all factor in, but as a rule the season most people dread is the one experienced locals look forward to.

**Planning tools and technique.** Practical planning means identifying the controlling current station for your site, checking the exchange size, and timing your entry so you are in the water before slack and out before the current rebuilds. On current-sensitive shore dives, smart divers use the water rather than fight it — riding a gentle current out and timing the reversal to carry them back to their exit. The specifics live in each site's entry in Part 7 and in local resources, but the habit is universal: plan the current first, everything else second.

**Two local resources worth bookmarking.** You do not have to do any of this from scratch. [pnwdiving.com](https://pnwdiving.com) is a community hub built around exactly this problem: divers post visibility reports for sites across Washington, Oregon, and British Columbia, so you can see how a site has been looking before you make the drive; and you are encouraged to submit your own report after a dive, which is what keeps the whole thing useful for everyone. It also gathers tide-and-current planning tools and a dive-site directory in one place (see its [resources page](https://pnwdiving.com/resources)). [planyourdive.com](https://planyourdive.com) complements it with site-by-site planning: a map covering most of the dive sites in the area, each with its own details, slack predictions, and a tide calendar, so you can find a workable slack window for a specific site at a glance. Treat both as starting points rather than gospel: always cross-check the prediction against the controlling current station and your own read of the day — but together they take most of the friction out of planning a dive here.[^planning-tools]

**Where this meets the safety principle.** Reading the water is also where "dive within your training level" stops being abstract. The honest call to cancel a dive because the exchange is too big, or to turn around when conditions exceed what you trained for, is the exact judgment your training is supposed to build. No octopus is worth a dive you are not equipped to make.

**A repeatable pre-dive checklist.** Before any current-sensitive dive: confirm the controlling current station and predicted slack; check the exchange size and whether it fits your skill; plan entry, turnaround, and exit around the current, not the clock; verify your exposure protection and gas plan for the temperature and depth; and agree clear go/no-go criteria with your buddy before you suit up.

**A funny side effect.** All of this preparation rewires you, and it never quite switches off. Between the exposure protection, the weighting, the tank choice, the slack windows, and the checklists, there is so much to prepare for a Salish Sea dive that when I go back to the tropics — where the whole routine is a thin wetsuit and a giant stride off the boat — I catch myself running through my mental list and wondering what I am missing. The answer is usually nothing; warm water simply asks for less. I have come to see that nagging thoroughness as a quiet gift of cold-water diving: the habits it builds travel with you, and they make you a more careful, more capable diver everywhere else in the world you dive.

### Key takeaways

Plan the current before you plan the scenery. Tide height is not the same thing as current speed, and slack is local. Use planning tools, then cross-check them with the actual site, the day, and your buddy's comfort. A canceled dive is not wasted judgment; it is judgment doing its job.

## Part 6 — Underwater Life

### Summary

This is the payoff. Cold, green water turns out to host some of the most densely populated diving on earth, and the life here is nothing like a tropical reef: a giant octopus, fish with almost human faces, devoted fathers guarding eggs, sponges older than you are, and a jewelry box of color hiding in the gloom. Here is a glimpse of who you will be sharing the water with. Everything in this part I have met in my home waters of Puget Sound — and the same animals range across the wider Salish Sea.

### In depth

From the surface, Puget Sound looks like nothing: flat, gray-green, opaque. That is the great misdirection. The same cold, nutrient-rich water that hides the view feeds an explosion of life, and much of it is big, strange, and entirely unbothered by you. What follows is only a partial introduction: the animals that, for me, make pulling on a drysuit in January worth it every single time.

#### The headliners

The **giant Pacific octopus** is the animal that put this region on the map for divers: the largest octopus species on earth, with an arm span that can exceed twice your height, and intelligent enough to recognize individual divers and unscrew jars.[^marine-life] It spends most of its life tucked into a rocky den, given away by the midden of cracked crab and clam shells at the entrance. Far more often, though, you will meet its smaller cousin — the **red octopus**, palm-sized, rust-colored, and forever pouring itself over the bottom into the next crevice.

<figure class="wrap-left">
<img src="img/young_wolf_eel.jpg" alt="A juvenile wolf eel in orange coloring">
<figcaption>A young wolf eel, in the brilliant orange of its juvenile years.</figcaption>
</figure>

A few feet away, two faces may be watching you from a crack in the rock. **Wolf eels** are not eels at all but long, heavy-bodied fish with jaws built for crushing urchins and crabs, and a face it is hard to call anything but expressive. They often live as mated pairs in a single den (you met one such pair back in Part 1), and despite the prizefighter looks they are gentle and curious. The young are a different animal entirely: a brilliant freckled orange, before they grow into their gray, craggy middle age.

#### The fish

<figure><img src="img/g/rockfish.jpg" alt="rockfish"><figcaption>A rockfish — slow-growing and long-lived</figcaption></figure>

<figure><img src="img/g/ratfish2.jpg" alt="spotted ratfish"><figcaption>A spotted ratfish, an ancient shark relative</figcaption></figure>
</div>

The **lingcod** is the resident ambush predator: a big, mottled fish that holds dead still on a ledge until something smaller swims too close. In winter the males turn into devoted fathers, fanning and guarding pale egg masses wedged into the rocks, and they will square up to a diver who drifts too near. **Rockfish** are the elders of the reef: slow-growing and site-attached, with some species astonishingly long-lived; an old fish on a familiar ledge may well be older than the diver looking at it. Then come the characters: the **sailfin sculpin** raises a comically tall, banded dorsal sail; the **cabezon** is a giant sculpin that shifts color to match the rock; and the **spotted ratfish** — an ancient cousin of the sharks, with iridescent green eyes and a smooth, ghostly glide — is the one that most makes you feel you have left the ordinary ocean behind.

#### The living reef

<div class="gallery">
<figure><img src="img/g/cloud_sponges.jpg" alt="cloud sponge"><figcaption>Cloud sponge — a glass sponge, decades to centuries old</figcaption></figure>
<figure><img src="img/g/ling_cod_eggs_and_cloud_sponges.jpg" alt="lingcod eggs beneath cloud sponges"><figcaption>A lingcod egg mass guarded beneath the sponges</figcaption></figure>

Much of what you will photograph is not going anywhere. **Cloud sponges** — pale, antler-like glass sponges — grow only a little each year and can be decades to centuries old; the Salish Sea is one of the very few places on earth where they still build reefs.[^marine-life] Pilings and wrecks vanish under white and orange **plumose anemones**, waving like feathery gardens in the current. And the **sea stars** are a story in themselves: the many-armed **sunflower star**, once everywhere and then nearly erased by a wasting epidemic, is a genuinely moving sight when you find a healthy one today, while the blue-and-orange **sun stars** add color to the gloom.[^sunflower-star]

#### Jewels and drifters

<div class="gallery">
<figure><img src="img/g/nudibranches.jpg" alt="nudibranch"><figcaption>A nudibranch — one of hundreds of local species</figcaption></figure>
<figure><img src="img/g/SportDiver_20260605_155140.jpg" alt="sea slug"><figcaption>Another sea slug, grazing the bottom</figcaption></figure>
<figure><img src="img/g/uburubur2.jpg" alt="jellyfish"><figcaption>A jellyfish drifting through the green</figcaption></figure>
<figure><img src="img/g/Crab.jpg" alt="crab"><figcaption>A decorator crab in the shell hash</figcaption></figure>
</div>

Look closer and the reef becomes a jewelry box. **Nudibranchs** — shell-less sea slugs in electric oranges, pinks, and whites — are so varied and so photogenic that half the divers here end up macro-obsessed. Overhead, the water column drifts with **jellyfish**, from delicate clear moon jellies to the big amber lion's mane trailing its curtain of tentacles. And the bottom stays busy with **crabs**: fist-sized Dungeness shouldering through the silt, and decorator crabs that glue bits of the reef onto their own backs as camouflage.

#### If you're lucky

Some of the best encounters you cannot plan for — and several of them are warm-blooded. **Harbor seals** are the common surprise: bold and playful, they will often appear behind you, tug a fin or hang in the water studying your bubbles, then vanish before you have finished turning around. Less often, and far more heart-stopping, a **sea lion** buzzes the dive: California and Steller sea lions are big, fast, and astonishingly agile, and having one rocket past your mask, twist around, and come back for another look is the kind of thing that has divers surfacing mid-laugh. Either one turns an ordinary dive into a story you tell for weeks.

The legend of these waters, though, is the **bluntnose sixgill shark** — and I will admit up front that I have yet to see one. It is a true deep-sea animal, a living fossil with six gill slits instead of the usual five and none of the tall dorsal fin you picture on a shark, normally cruising thousands of feet down. But Puget Sound appears to be a nursery for them, and through the summer months, roughly June into September, the smaller ones sometimes rise into recreational depth. They are not exclusive to one site; deep slopes and channels around the Sound can produce stories and sightings. **Redondo Beach** in Des Moines is simply the best-known local example for recreational divers, because the bottom drops from about 25 to 100 feet in a way that mimics their deep-water home. Sightings there have become reliable enough that the city of Des Moines has declared itself the **"Sixgill Shark Capital of the World,"** named July 6 *Sixgill Shark Day*, and state lawmakers are now pushing to make the bluntnose sixgill Washington's official state shark. For all their size (adults can reach perhaps eighteen feet, though the Sound's juveniles run nearer six), they are generally described as elusive deep-water animals rather than a routine threat to divers, and people who have swum with them talk about it as one of the great encounters in cold-water diving.[^sixgill] One of these summers, I intend to be one of them.

None of it is guaranteed, which is exactly what keeps you coming back. This is the reward the cold protects — and the reason the planning, the training, and the heavy gear in the rest of this guide are worth it. The next part is about where to go to find them.

### Key takeaways

The Salish Sea rewards attention. Big encounters are wonderful, but much of the magic is small, slow, and easily missed: a nudibranch on a piling, eggs guarded under a ledge, a red octopus tucked into a crack. Move gently, control your buoyancy, and treat the dive as a visit rather than a collection trip.

## Part 7 — Where to Dive

### Summary

The local sites sort naturally by how much tide planning they demand, which maps cleanly onto skill and training level. Tier 1 sites are diveable almost anytime and ideal for newcomers; Tier 2 sites add occasional current; Tier 3 sites are solidly intermediate; and Tier 4 sites are demanding, current-swept dives that have killed unprepared divers. The progression is the point — resist jumping tiers.

### In depth

The single best way to think about where to dive here is to order sites by current sensitivity. The tiers below double as a training progression: a new diver belongs in Tier 1, earns their way into Tier 2 and 3 with experience and specialty training, and treats Tier 4 as something to grow into deliberately, never to sample early.[^dive-sites]

Before you use any site description here, verify the current access rules, tide and current predictions, weather, visibility reports, parking, and local hazards. Sites change, rules change, and conditions can turn a familiar place into a different dive. Treat this list as a planning map, not permission.

**Tier 1 — Easiest, dive almost anytime (true beginner).**

- **Cove 2, with Cove 1 and Cove 3** (Seacrest Park, West Seattle). The region's training site, minutes from downtown — and what one local instructor only half-jokingly calls "the World's Greatest Dive Site" (WGDS). A moderate slope leads past recreational limits, the silty bottom is dotted with submerged logs, pilings, and the small *Honey Bear* wreck, all encrusted with life, and there is a Seattle skyline view and restrooms on site. It is among the most current-insensitive sites near the city, and a protected one that stays calm even when rough weather makes more exposed sites unworkable, which is exactly why you will see so many shops training and operating here. Seattle Dive Tours runs regular guided local dives at the cove, and Argonaut Diving, Silent World, Underwater Sports, and others run their training here too. Watch the water-taxi exclusion zone and the crowds of fellow students.
- **Sund Rock** (Hood Canal). A privately maintained marine preserve near Hoodsport — closed to fishing and entered by reservation — and, to my mind, one of the best sites in the region for a newer diver. Because nothing is harvested here, the wildlife has rebounded spectacularly: it is about the closest thing to a "guaranteed critters" dive there is, with large resident giant Pacific octopus and adult wolf eels, often in mated pairs, alongside lingcod, rockfish, nudibranchs, and plumose anemones. It is also genuinely beginner-friendly: an easy shore entry, little to no current so you can dive almost any time, and a shallow basin between the north and south walls that keeps you comfortably within recreational depths, while the walls themselves drop well past those limits for divers who want more. There are restrooms and a warm shower on site.

**Tier 2 — Some current at times (beginner to intermediate).**

- **Sunnyside Beach** (Steilacoom). A friendly South Sound shore dive on a long sandy beach, with full facilities (restrooms, outdoor showers, picnic areas) and a small cash parking fee. Tucked away from the ferocious currents of the nearby Tacoma Narrows, it offers an easy entry, a gently sloping sandy bottom, and few protruding hazards, which is why it is so widely used as a beginner and training site (you cross a set of railroad tracks to reach the water, so use caution). Two features anchor the dive: an old offshore pipeline that reliably gathers life, and a couple of small wrecks off the pilings — both home to resident octopus, sculpin, rockfish, and anemones, with seasonal surprises. Visibility is usually modest, which makes it honest practice for local conditions. It is also where I completed my own divemaster project.
- **Mukilteo (T-Dock).** One of my own favorite sites: accessible and usually calm, with surprisingly diverse life under and around the old dock. A sunken geodesic dome, the "geodome," anchors the dive and is a reliable spot to find juvenile wolf eels and red octopuses among beautiful stands of plumose anemones. It is also a home base for several north-end shops, including Evergreen Dive Service, Lighthouse Diving Center, and Underwater Sports. Just monitor your gauges, as the muck drops into depth quickly.
- **Edmonds Underwater Park.** A shallow no-take marine reserve packed with oversized lingcod, cabezon, and rockfish, diveable in most moderate tidal exchanges.
- **Redondo Beach** (Federal Way). An easy, reliable everyday site that can nonetheless throw surprisingly strong, odd currents.
- **Les Davis** (Tacoma). A waterfront artificial reef and one of the standard "dive anytime" sites — and a genuine piece of local history: the reef is built largely from salvaged sections of the original Tacoma Narrows Bridge, the infamous "Galloping Gertie" that twisted apart in a 1940 windstorm. Those concrete blocks now shelter lingcod, rockfish, and octopus. It also serves as a home base for dive shops on the south side, including the Tacoma branch of Underwater Sports and YSS Dive.
- **Keystone Jetty** (Fort Casey, Whidbey Island). One of the most highly regarded sites in the state and regularly used for courses, but with real current rules: dive around slack before flood, stay on the east side of the jetty, and never round the spit into the ferry lane. The cluster of pilings makes a calmer second dive.

**Tier 3 — Intermediate.**

- **Saltwater State Park** (Des Moines). Sunken structures and an artificial reef, rewarding once you can manage moderate exchanges.
- **Octopus Hole** (Hood Canal). Rock walls dropping to 70–80 feet hold lingcod, giant Pacific octopus, wolf eels, and nudibranchs. It is primarily a boat or private-beach access site, though there is a roadside pullout many divers use for shore access.

**Tier 4 — Advanced and demanding.**

- **Sunrise Beach Park** (Gig Harbor). A beautiful sandstone reef full of wolf eels and octopus — and an extremely current-intensive site where divers have died. It must be done on corrected slack, ideally when the Tacoma Narrows runs under about two knots on both sides of the exchange, and it adds a long, steep gear hike from the parking lot. This is not a site to attempt outside your training and slack-planning competence.
- **Flagpole** (near the Tacoma Narrows / Day Island). A near-guaranteed wolf-eel site, but current-swept and Narrows-influenced, demanding tight slack timing.
- **Tacoma Narrows / Day Island Wall.** Ledges and walls from 40 to 100 feet loaded with wolf eels, diveable only at slack on a small exchange under roughly three knots because of the Narrows' powerful currents. Think of it as a graduation dive.

**Diving from a boat — local charters.** A lot of the best diving in the Sound (deeper walls, current-swept pinnacles, and sites with no beach to walk in from) is reachable only by boat. A handful of local dive charters fill that gap; **Sea Ya**, **Bandito Charters**, **YSS Dive Charters**, **PNW Dive Tours**, and **Pallin Around Charters** are a few of the names you will hear, among others. You can sometimes book a seat directly, but just as often you will find one through your local dive shop or your club, which charter the whole boat for a day and fill it with members. It is the natural way to expand your range once the shore sites start to feel familiar, and it opens up a side of Puget Sound most divers never see.

It is also a real step up in self-reliance, and you should be genuinely comfortable in the water before you take it. A boat dive is not a shore dive with a different parking lot. Entries are giant strides or back-rolls straight into open water; descents and ascents happen on a line or in blue water with no bottom to orient to; many sites are run as drifts on a tight slack window; and your exit is a ladder you have to locate and climb in full gear — sometimes in chop, sometimes in current, sometimes after surfacing well away from the boat. There is no standing up and walking out. That demands solid buoyancy and trim, reliable gas planning, confident navigation, and the habit of carrying and deploying an SMB so the boat can find you. Dial those in on the easy shore sites first; then the boat becomes the doorway to the rest of the region.

**Beyond these shores.** These are only a small snippet of the region's dive sites: a starting set chosen because they are accessible, well known, and sort cleanly by difficulty. The Salish Sea holds dozens more, and the greater region keeps going well past it: the same cold, green, life-packed water stretches north up the coast of British Columbia, where some of the finest cold-water diving on earth waits for divers willing to travel for it.

The headliner is **God's Pocket**, an off-grid dive lodge on Hurst Island off the northern tip of Vancouver Island, reachable only by boat or float plane from Port Hardy. Its world-famous **Browning Pass** sits where deep-ocean currents upwell into nutrient-rich shallows, carpeting the walls in red soft corals, sponges, and fields of giant white plumose anemones — a density of life that rivals anywhere on the planet. It is also genuinely demanding: ripping currents, drift dives and tight slack windows, variable visibility, and no in-water guide, so you plan and run your own dives after the crew's briefing. The resort accordingly requires a minimum of 100 logged dives (at least 50 of them in cold water and a drysuit), along with nitrox certification and real comfort in current.[^gods-pocket] In other words, it is exactly the kind of place the rest of this guide is quietly preparing you for: dive the Sound well, build the numbers and the cold-water hours, and a trip like God's Pocket becomes a goal you are ready for rather than a risk you take too early.

### Key takeaways

Choose sites by current sensitivity first, not by fame. Tier 1 is where you build comfort, Tier 2 and 3 are where you broaden range, and Tier 4 is where experience, slack planning, and humility all need to show up together. The site you skip today will still be there when you are ready.

## Part 8 — What Comes Next

### Summary

Once you can comfortably dive the Sound, the question shifts from *how do I start* to *how far do I want to go*. The certification ladder — Open Water, Advanced Open Water, Rescue, and onward into specialties and the professional ranks — is the map of that progression. Each rung is a real shift in capability, not just another card, and climbing it deliberately, never ahead of your training, is what keeps a growing diver a safe one.

### In depth

Everything up to this point is about getting into the water here and diving it well. This final part looks ahead: where you can take your diving once the basics are second nature. It is not a prerequisite for getting started — it is the road that opens up afterward.

The recreational ladder is not just a sequence of cards. Each level marked a genuine shift in how I related to the water, and it is worth saying what they have come to mean to me.

**Open Water** is where you start figuring out yourself. The entire focus at this stage is *you*: proving that you are safe, that you can breathe underwater, and that your gear works and you know how to use it. The skills are foundational — clearing your mask, recovering a regulator, controlling a slow descent and ascent, sharing air with a buddy, planning a simple dive to recreational limits around 60 feet (18 meters).[^training-depths] You are not yet thinking about the scenery; you are learning to be calm and self-sufficient in an environment humans were never built for. That is more than enough for one certification.

**Advanced Open Water** is where, now comfortable with yourself in the water, you begin to enjoy the environment — and to me that is what the Advanced course is really for. It lets you perfect your buoyancy, and as someone once recommended to me, I strongly recommend taking Peak Performance Buoyancy as one of your adventure dives. Good buoyancy is environmental stewardship: it is being aware of where you put your feet, your hands, and your body, so you do not crush a fragile anemone, snap a sponge, or kick up a cloud of silt over the reef and the life you came to see. It is the difference between visiting the underwater world and damaging it. Advanced also takes you deeper, toward the 100-foot (30-meter) range, opening up the creatures that live in the deeper, darker water, and it sharpens your underwater navigation so you can actually find your way around a site.

**Rescue** is where your focus turns outward. By this stage you should feel genuinely confident in your own skills and be enjoying the environment far more than when you started, which frees up the attention to look after everyone else. Rescue Diver is widely considered the most demanding and the most rewarding recreational course, because it teaches you to anticipate and manage trouble: recognizing a stressed or panicked diver, assisting a tired one, handling an unresponsive diver at the surface and from depth, running search patterns, and coordinating an emergency. This is the moment a diver grows up: the shift from "me and my environment" to caring about the other divers around you and the team you dive with.

**Divemaster** is the first step onto the professional path. Once managing emergencies feels like second nature, the natural next move is to become a dive leader, and Divemaster is where that begins. It deepens your dive theory, raises your own skills to demonstration quality, and teaches you to guide certified divers, assist instructors with their students, and run the logistics of a dive — including mapping a site from scratch, which is exactly what my own divemaster project at Sunnyside Beach involved (Part 7). It is the bridge between diving for yourself and taking responsibility for others in the water.

Beyond that core ladder, the path keeps climbing the same way it began — one trained step at a time. After my drysuit and nitrox training at Underwater Sports in Seattle, I went on to a Deep specialty with Argonaut Diving, then solo diving and closed-circuit rebreather work with Silent World, a diver propulsion vehicle course with YSS Dive, the Master Scuba Diver rating, an introduction to technical diving with Tec 40, and finally the divemaster rating through Evergreen Dive Service. Each step earned access to something new, and never the other way around. The configurations and shops behind those steps come in Parts 3 and 4.

There is also a whole menu of specialty certifications you can add as your interests sharpen, and a few of them have earned a specific recommendation for this region.

**Enriched Air (Nitrox)** is the one I would take first; around here it is near-essential alongside drysuit. It is worth being precise about what it is and what it is not. Enriched air is breathing gas with a higher fraction of oxygen than regular air (commonly 32 or 36 percent), which means less nitrogen. Less nitrogen means your body loads it more slowly, and that buys you longer no-decompression time at the moderate depths where so much local diving happens. What it is *not* is a deep-diving gas: the extra oxygen actually imposes a *shallower* maximum operating depth, because oxygen becomes toxic under enough pressure, and the course exists largely to teach you to respect that limit. It also does not make your tank last longer; you breathe it at exactly the same rate.[^nitrox] The payoff is time, and here that time matters: many of the region's best sites sit deep enough that air runs you into no-deco limits early, and on a summer dive at Redondo's bottle field, a seasonal destination during sixgill months, enriched air is what lets you settle in at depth and wait for that shadow to pass instead of watching your computer tick down. The same applies to lingering anywhere in the deeper, darker range where some of the most interesting residents live.

**Deep Diver** extends your training to the full recreational limit of 130 feet, and this region gives you real reasons to use it: wonderful walls — Day Island, Sund Rock's drop-offs, and the boat-access walls and pinnacles — fall away well past 100 feet.[^training-depths] The honest caveat is that depth is a tool, not a destination. In these green waters most of the life concentrates shallower than about 60 feet, where the light still reaches, so the skill is knowing *when* a dive is worth taking deep, and pairing it with nitrox and disciplined gas planning when it is.

**Diver Propulsion Vehicle (DPV)** changes the scale of a dive. With a scooter you cover far more range with far less effort, exploring a site quicker and wider than you ever could on fin power: a long pipeline run, the full sweep of a wall, or the far structure you have only seen on a map. It is enormous fun and a genuine capability, but it also moves you away from your exit faster than your legs ever would, which is exactly why it is taught as its own course (YSS Dive runs one locally, as covered in Part 3).

**Underwater Photography** might be the most rewarding of all here, because the Salish Sea is a photographer's sea: nudibranchs, wolf-eel portraits, octopus eyes, and sponge gardens that reward a macro lens more than almost anywhere. Every photograph in this guide came from exactly this path. The course is about more than camera settings; it teaches you to manage lighting and composition in green, particle-filled water, and, just as importantly, to hold flawless, motionless buoyancy while your attention is on the viewfinder, so you get the shot without crushing the reef or silting out the scene. Fair warning that a camera is task loading, so let your buoyancy become second nature before you bring one. The reward runs the other way too: your photos become dive logs, species records, and the thing that finally convinces your skeptical friends that all of this lives under that gray-green surface.

Beyond those, the menu continues — navigation, night, wreck, search and recovery, sidemount, and onward toward technical diving and rebreathers. None of them is mandatory; you take the ones that match the diving you actually want to do, each as its own piece of training.

**One skill at a time.** If it sounds like I have said this throughout the guide, it is because I have, and it bears saying once more here: take your time. Everything in diving is a progression, and none of it is worth rushing. Every specialty you add is another layer of task loading — one more thing complicating the dive that you must stay on top of, the whole dive long. Stack them and the load compounds fast: running a DPV while shooting underwater photography on a CCR means managing the scooter, the camera, and the rebreather all at once, each with its own attention demands and its own ways of going wrong, on top of the buoyancy, gas, navigation, and buddy awareness every dive already requires. The divers who get there safely are the ones who build one skill at a time, letting each become second nature before adding the next. That is the whole philosophy of this guide distilled into a single habit: let your training lead, and the diving follows safely.

### Key takeaways

The ladder is not about collecting cards. Each course should answer a real need in the diving you are actually doing: warmer, safer, deeper, longer, more independent, or more responsible for others. Add one skill, practice it until it is boring, then decide whether the next one still matters.

If there is one idea to carry out of this guide, it is the one that opened it: the divers who thrive in the Salish Sea are the ones who respect their limits and expand them on purpose, exactly the way a certification path is designed to work. My own route through all of it was never about collecting cards. It was about earning, step by careful step, the right to dive a place this demanding and this rewarding.

Do that, and the Salish Sea opens up: a cold, green, living world most people will never know is right here in our backyard. Train for it, respect it, and it will give you a lifetime of diving.
